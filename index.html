The contractor who spent a decade building his own system

Field note 01 · Systems & integration
Field → internal app → accounting

One of the clearest signals we heard in discovery came from a contractor who had already spent roughly a decade building an application around his business.

His system connected field information, invoice documents, and QuickBooks. The work was not about choosing a new platform, but about moving information between existing ones without losing context.

The software was evidence

Businesses rarely maintain their own application for years unless the workflow gap is persistent. The tool itself showed that the existing software did not fully close the handoffs between work performed and records kept.

What we took from it

A useful assistant can sit between systems people already trust. It can carry context forward while leaving accountability and the source of truth in place.

The lesson: the strongest workflow signal is often the workaround a business has already built.

When the owner is still the integration layer

Field note 02 · Owner operations
Messages → owner → decisions

In a small contracting business, calls, crew updates, client decisions, invoices, and scheduling can all be organized in different places. The owner often has to reconcile what they mean together.

The bottleneck is context reconstruction

A commitment in a text may affect a schedule and a payment. Someone has to remember that relationship, even when the individual tools are working correctly.

What we took from it

Rather than make the owner document more, the better system reconstructs business context from approved communication and surfaces only the decisions that need attention.

The lesson: sometimes the integration layer is a person, not software.

The right problem can belong to the wrong person

Field note 03 · Discovery & roles
Buyer and operator are different roles

A discovery conversation can end with an owner who says somebody else handles the administrative workflow. That answer identifies a question that matters more than the owner’s personal interest.

Do not confuse buyer and user

The project manager, administrator, or office lead may be doing the reconciliation, chasing, and updating. They experience the friction directly, while the owner sees only the outcome.

What we took from it

A responsible pilot should involve the person operating the process, not simply the person who approves spending.

The lesson: a no from one person can mean that the workflow belongs to someone else.

When Procore already solves the problem

Field note 04 · Existing software
Finding the boundary of a working platform

One contractor was already using Procore and could not identify a meaningful unmet need in the workflow we were discussing. That is useful product information.

Discovery is not objection handling

The question is not how to replace a trusted platform. It is whether important communication and decisions still live outside it and require manual reconciliation.

What we took from it

Where the existing system covers the work, an additional product needs a specific, demonstrable gap. If there is no gap, there may be no job to do.

The lesson: sometimes the existing software is doing its job.

When good enough is actually good enough

Field note 05 · Product fit
Potential benefit versus switching cost

A contractor we contacted said Housecall Pro was already working well. There was no clear recurring problem demanding a new layer.

The threshold is operational pain

New software brings setup, attention, trust, and behavior-change costs. A theoretically clever feature cannot compensate for an absent problem.

What we took from it

Look for repeatable evidence: missed follow-ups, buried decisions, duplicate entry, or manual reconstruction. Otherwise, leave the workflow alone.

The lesson: the absence of pain is an answer.

Interest is not the same as ownership

Field note 06 · Pilot qualification
Interest → owner → criteria

Early interest can lead to a meeting. It does not by itself identify the information to track, the failure points to address, or the outcome that would make a pilot worthwhile.

A pilot needs a workflow owner

Somebody inside the operation must know which channels matter, how decisions happen, and what should count as useful output.

What we took from it

The right design partner can describe the work, show where it breaks, and judge the prototype against real operating needs.

The lesson: a clear operating owner matters more than vague enthusiasm.
